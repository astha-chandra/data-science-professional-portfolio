Project 01 · Data Science Project

Customer Churn Prediction & Retention Intelligence

An end-to-end machine learning project developed to identify customers at elevated risk of churn and translate model outputs into practical retention decision support.

Python pandas scikit-learn Logistic Regression Random Forest Power BI

Business Challenge

The project explored whether customer information could be used to identify customers who were more likely to leave and whether those predictions could be translated into useful retention priorities.

Core question: Can churn risk be predicted with sufficient reliability to support customer retention decisions, while explicitly balancing the cost of missed churners against unnecessary retention interventions?

The dataset contained 10,000 customer records, with an observed churn rate of approximately 20.37%. Because the churn class was substantially smaller than the non-churn class, model evaluation focused on more than overall accuracy. Churn recall, F1 score and ROC-AUC were considered alongside accuracy.

Data Infrastructure & Tools

The project used a lightweight analytical stack suited to a structured public dataset of 10,000 customer records. Tool selection was based on reproducibility, modelling requirements, interpretability and the need to translate technical outputs into accessible decision support.

Python & pandas

Python and pandas were used for data inspection, cleaning, transformation and analytical preparation. For a dataset of this scale, in-memory processing provided a transparent and efficient workflow without requiring distributed infrastructure.

scikit-learn

scikit-learn was selected for preprocessing pipelines, model training and evaluation because the project involved conventional supervised classification on structured tabular data. Pipelines also helped keep preprocessing consistent between training and test data.

Power BI

Power BI was used after modelling to translate predictions and evaluation outputs into an interactive decision-support layer. This separated the technical modelling workflow from the information required by operational users, such as customer risk, model performance and retention priorities.

Why this architecture was proportionate

A local Python workflow was proportionate for a static dataset of 10,000 observations and supported rapid experimentation. Introducing distributed processing or cloud-scale infrastructure would have added complexity without a corresponding analytical benefit at this scale.

Scalability limitation: This architecture should not be interpreted as a production design. If the solution were applied to substantially larger customer volumes, multiple live data sources or frequent scoring requirements, the local workflow would become increasingly difficult to operate reliably. A production implementation would require automated ingestion and validation, versioned model artefacts, scheduled or event-driven scoring, controlled deployment and ongoing performance and data-drift monitoring.
Alternative approaches: Distributed processing platforms and cloud-based machine-learning services could provide greater scalability and automation, but were not required for the scale or purpose of this project. Similarly, Power BI was appropriate for stakeholder-facing exploration, whereas a production application requiring real-time individual predictions would require a different serving layer. Tool choice was therefore treated as dependent on the analytical and operational context rather than as a one-size-fits-all technology decision.

Data Engineering & Preprocessing Decisions

Data preparation was designed to support fair out-of-sample evaluation and reproducible model scoring. Preprocessing choices were made with particular attention to class balance, consistency between training and test data, and avoidance of information leakage.

Stratified train-test split

The dataset was divided into 80% training and 20% test data using stratification on the churn target. This preserved the minority churn proportion across both samples and reduced the risk that model comparison would be distorted by materially different class distributions.

Pipeline-based preprocessing

Numerical scaling and categorical encoding were incorporated within scikit-learn pipelines. This ensured that transformations were learned from the training data and then applied consistently to the hold-out test set, rather than being fitted independently to the complete dataset.

Categorical encoding

One-hot encoding was used for categorical features because the categories did not represent a meaningful ordinal scale. The handle_unknown="ignore" setting also reduced the risk of scoring failure if a previously unseen category appeared during inference.

Feature engineering

Interpretable derived features such as AgeBand were created to improve analysis and dashboard communication. Derived operational fields such as ChurnProbability, PredictedChurn and retention actions were created only after model scoring and were not used as predictors.

Leakage control: Outcome-derived fields and model outputs were excluded from the predictor set. The hold-out test set was retained for evaluation rather than being used to tune preprocessing choices or engineer features around final test outcomes. This helps ensure that reported performance reflects behaviour on unseen data rather than information inadvertently carried forward from the target.
Alternative preprocessing choices: Standardisation was particularly useful for Logistic Regression, whereas Random Forest models are less sensitive to feature scale. A separate tree-specific pipeline without scaling could therefore have been used. Similarly, different age bands or no age-band feature at all would have been reasonable alternatives. These choices may influence interpretability and model behaviour, so they should be validated rather than assumed to improve generalisation.
Generalisation consideration: The preprocessing pipeline is appropriate for the observed Kaggle dataset, but a production version would need validation against new customer populations, changing category distributions and evolving data-quality rules. Monitoring for missing fields, unseen categories, churn-rate shifts and feature distribution drift would therefore be required before automated scoring.

Project Workflow

1. Data preparation
Customer data was reviewed, cleaned and transformed into model-ready features. Interpretable fields such as AgeBand were also created to support analysis and dashboard communication.
2. Train-test design
A stratified 80:20 split was used, producing 8,000 training observations and 2,000 test observations while preserving the churn distribution.
3. Preprocessing
Numerical variables were standardised and categorical variables were one-hot encoded inside scikit-learn pipelines to support consistent training and scoring.
4. Model comparison
Logistic Regression, Random Forest and a reduced-feature Random Forest were evaluated using out-of-sample performance metrics.
5. Threshold optimisation
Alternative probability thresholds were assessed to improve churn recall and identify a more useful operational balance between precision and recall.
6. Business intelligence
Predictions and risk information were integrated into Power BI to provide operational monitoring, model-performance reporting and model interpretation.

Model Comparison

Multiple approaches were compared so that the final model choice was based on evidence rather than on a single algorithm.

Model Accuracy ROC-AUC Interpretation
Logistic Regression 82.65% 79.95% Useful baseline, but lower discrimination and churn recall.
Random Forest 85.80% 85.28% Stronger overall predictive performance.
Reduced Random Forest 86.00% 85.03% Similar performance with a more focused feature set.
Random Forest was retained as the basis for the operational solution because it provided stronger predictive discrimination than Logistic Regression while still supporting post-model interpretation through permutation importance.

Threshold Optimisation

The default 0.50 classification threshold was not accepted automatically. At that threshold the model produced relatively high churn precision but identified fewer genuine churners. Lower thresholds were therefore tested to investigate the operational trade-off.

Threshold Precision Recall F1
0.50 76.57% 44.96% 56.66%
0.40 66.97% 54.30% 59.97%
0.35 62.12% 60.44% 61.27%
0.30 55.88% 66.58% 60.76%
Decision: A threshold of 0.35 was selected because it produced the highest F1 score among the thresholds tested while substantially improving recall compared with the default 0.50 threshold.

Final Operational Model

84.45% Accuracy
85.03% ROC-AUC
60.44% Churn Recall
61.27% Churn F1
Final model: Random Forest with a decision threshold of 0.35.

Business interpretation

The model identified 246 of 407 actual churners, corresponding to 60.44% recall.

The lower threshold also resulted in 150 customers who ultimately stayed being flagged for retention activity.

This represents a deliberate trade-off: more genuine churners are surfaced for intervention, but additional retention capacity may be required.

Limitations & Responsible Use

Model limitations

Results are based on one historical public dataset and should not be treated as guaranteed production performance. Churn behaviour, customer mix and data quality may change over time.

Responsible use

Age, gender and geography may create fairness concerns in a real banking environment. Predictions should support proportionate human-reviewed retention decisions rather than automated adverse treatment of customers.

Monitoring

A deployed model would require monitoring of churn prevalence, score distributions, recall, ROC-AUC, calibration and segment-level performance.

Business validation

Predictive performance does not prove that a retention intervention prevents churn. Controlled testing would be required to measure incremental business impact.

What I Learned

This project reinforced that model selection cannot be based on accuracy alone. The most useful operating point depends on the business consequences of different error types.

It also demonstrated the importance of connecting technical model outputs to accessible decision-support tools. The strongest solution was not simply the model with the best score, but the combination of predictive modelling, threshold evaluation, interpretation and operational visualisation.
